==============================================================================
5  THE LENGTH OF AN OWNED PERIOD, BY THE INTERVAL ITS DECISION CHOSE
==============================================================================
A period runs from one resolution to the next of the same pair.  The send it
holds is due one applied interval after the PREVIOUS send, so the length is that
interval less the time the resolution itself had already taken, plus the lag of
the next resolution.  After an ANSWERED poll the resolution follows its send by
about a tick, so the length sits just above the interval.  After a TIMED OUT poll
the resolution comes about 2 s after the send, so the period starts 2 s late and
the length sits about 2 s BELOW the interval -- unless the next poll times out
too, which pays the same 2 s back at the far end.  Seconds.

==============================================================================
6  THE COST OF AN OWNED PERIOD, PER PERIOD AND PER SECOND
==============================================================================
Cost of a period = its waiting ms + W x N per in-window send, divided by N, so
the numbers are SCORE ms -- the unit the earlier scratch precheck's rate table
used.  The rate is the pooled sum of cost over the pooled sum of seconds and not
a mean of per-period rates, so one short period cannot dominate it.  Two row
sets: every owned period, and the ones whose period STARTS inside the scoring
window.  The second filter reads the decision's own instant only, so it cannot
keep one arm more often than another for having landed its send in the window,
which is what a caused-send filter does and the reason none is used here.
The cost is split into its poll half and its waiting half, because the two move
in opposite directions with the interval and the split is the whole mechanism.

So the earlier precheck's own rows are the first set, and the two moves away
from them are separable.
- Its FILTER moves the long arm's rate the most, because that filter keeps a
  pre-window decision -- one poll charge over a gap holding almost no record
  time -- far more often for the long arm, which is the defect the earlier
  verification named.  The move is visible in the ans_empty rows, where the early
  decisions of a run sit.
- The UNIT raises every timeout cell's level, because a period then holds the
  next poll's own 2 s of waiting instead of handing it to the next decision.
- At p 0.2 nothing is censored (part 1), so the gap/caused rows there are the
  earlier precheck's quantity exactly.  At p 0.4 the earlier precheck could not
  place 3.582 % of the cost (its censored residual, part 1), so its rates there
  are lower than these by construction and the two are not the same quantity.

So, reading by reading:
- The residual.  The earlier precheck left a censored record's lower bound
  unplaced, which was its whole residual.  Here that record waits at the hop it
  never crossed and part 1's residual is 0 ms in both cells under both rules.
  What is non-zero instead is the head and tail share of part 1, which the
  earlier gap partition did not have: its gaps began at a send, so the time
  before a pair's first decision sat inside its first gap.
- The decision-counted reading.  The earlier precheck found 3.695 s cheapest per
  DECISION in all five cells.  Part 6's mean-cost-per-period column reproduces
  that ordering, and it is the same artefact: a period pays one poll whatever
  interval it chose, so counting by decisions prices 3.695 s over 3.7 s the same
  as 10.043 s over 10 s.
- The time-fair reading per cell.  The earlier precheck's headline was that what
  separates is always that 3.695 s is the DEAREST per second.  Of the 10
